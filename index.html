Authorship Patterns in Astronomical Literature (1827–2025)

How has scientific collaboration changed in astronomy?

This project analyses 200 years of astronomical papers from the NASA ADS API, tracking how authorship has evolved from single researchers in the 1800s to large collaborations of hundreds in the modern era.

Resurrection of my 2012 analysis, extended to 2025 with fresh data.

Average Authors per Paper

The 1960 inflection point: from solo research (~1 author) to collaborative science. By 2024, averaging ~12 authors per paper. The steep curve mirrors the rise of Big Science.

Average & Maximum Authors (Log Scale)

Log scale reveals the exponential growth. The maximum spikes to 1,782 authors—record-breaking collaborations like LIGO dominate recent years. The gap between average and max widens over time.

Authorship Distribution

From 1827 to 2024: single-author papers (blue) fade to nearly nothing, while 5+ author papers (purple) dominate. The distribution flip happened around 1996.

Citation-Weighted Distribution

Same as above, but weighted by citations. Multi-author papers get disproportionately more attention. Collaborative work, especially in the 5+ category, is cited more heavily than solo efforts.

Research Population & Paper Output

Top: unique author count (427k over the dataset). Bottom: papers per year. From 1960 onward, authors grow faster than papers—evidence that collaboration requires more people.

Research Efficiency: Authors per Paper

The ratio of unique authors to papers—a measure of teamwork intensity. Flat until 1960, then climbing steeply. Modern astronomy requires more heads per paper than it did 70 years ago.

Key Findings

2012 Original Analysis

  • 1827–1960: Authorship remained flat at ~1 author per paper
  • 1960+: Sharp acceleration in co-authorship across all journals
  • By 2012: Single-author papers just 6% of the total (down from ~100% in 1827)
  • Largest paper: 770 co-authors (LIGO gravitational wave search, 2011)
  • 5+ author papers: Became the dominant category by 2012
  • Citation advantage: Multi-author papers are cited more frequently relative to their publication rate
