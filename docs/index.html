How Astronomy Got Collaborative

1827 to 2024

In 1827, astronomical papers were written by one person with a telescope and a lot of time. By 2024, the largest papers have thousands of authors working across continents and decades.

This is the story of how that happened. Tracked across 200 years, 153,000 papers, and 427,000 researchers.

The Evidence

What We Saw in 2012

  • For 133 years (1827-1960), authorship stayed flat. One author. Every paper.
  • Then it accelerated. By 2012, it was already up to 3.5 authors per paper.
  • Single-author papers were down to 6 percent. They were nearly extinct.
  • The largest papers already had 770 authors (LIGO, 2011).
  • Big papers were getting cited more. Science was rewarding collaboration.

What We See in 2024

  • Average: 12 authors per paper. That is 12x what it was in 1960.
  • Single-author: 3.5 percent. Practically extinct. A footnote.
  • The new normal: 5-plus author papers are 61.7 percent of all papers.
  • Extreme papers: The largest now has 1,782 authors. Not a mistake.
  • The turn: 1996 was when 5-plus author papers overtook single-author papers. That year changed how science works.
  • The cost: We need more people than ever to make papers. Specialisation went up. Coordination went up. The barrier to entry went up.
